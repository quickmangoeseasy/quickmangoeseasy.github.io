The Mango

One of the world's most widely enjoyed tropical fruits, the mango has a long history, hundreds of varieties, and a surprisingly interesting journey from tree to table.

QUICK FACTS

Meet the mango.

Before getting into its history and varieties, here are a few basic facts about this distinctive tropical fruit.

🌳

It grows on trees.

Mangoes grow on evergreen trees that can become quite large and productive. Their fruit develops after flowering and may take several months to mature.

🌎

It is grown worldwide.

Mango cultivation is especially important in tropical and subtropical regions, where warm temperatures support the fruit's growth.

🥭

There are many varieties.

Mangoes are not one uniform fruit. Varieties can differ significantly in size, shape, color, texture, aroma, sweetness, and fiber.

BOTANICAL BASICS

A tropical fruit with a surprisingly deep history.

The mango belongs to the genus Mangifera and the cashew family, Anacardiaceae. Its scientific name is Mangifera indica. The species has been cultivated for thousands of years and has become deeply connected with food traditions in many parts of the world.

Although modern mangoes come in countless forms, their common ancestor is associated with South Asia. From there, mango cultivation and trade spread into other regions and eventually across the globe.

HISTORY

Thousands of years in the making.

Mango cultivation has an extremely long history. Evidence and historical traditions connect the domestication of mangoes with the Indian subcontinent.

Over centuries, mangoes traveled through trade routes and cultural exchanges. The fruit eventually reached regions throughout Southeast Asia, Africa, and later the Americas.

Today, mangoes are cultivated in many warm regions around the world, making them one of the most recognizable tropical fruits.

Ancient cultivation

Mangoes have been cultivated in South Asia for thousands of years, becoming an important part of regional food and agricultural traditions.

Expansion across Asia

Cultivation spread through trade and migration, particularly into Southeast Asia, where mangoes developed into many distinctive regional varieties.

Global cultivation

Mango cultivation eventually expanded to tropical and subtropical areas in Africa, the Americas, and elsewhere.

The modern mango

Modern agriculture has made mangoes available to consumers far beyond their traditional growing regions.

VARIETIES

Not every mango tastes the same.

Different mango varieties have their own characteristics. Some are especially sweet, some are firm and tart, and others are prized for their smooth texture.

Alphonso

Known for its rich aroma, smooth texture, and sweet flavor. It is particularly associated with India and is highly valued during its season.

Tommy Atkins

A widely distributed commercial variety known for its firm flesh and durability during transport.

Kent

Often recognized for relatively low fiber and a sweet flavor. It is grown in several tropical agricultural regions.

Keitt

A large mango that can remain green on the outside even when ripe. It is known for juicy flesh and a sweet flavor.

Ataulfo

A smaller mango with golden-yellow flesh and a creamy texture. It is commonly sold in markets across North America.

Haden

A historically important variety that helped influence mango cultivation in parts of the Americas.

FROM FLOWER TO FRUIT

It all starts with a flowering tree.

Mango trees produce flowering structures that contain many small flowers. Only some flowers ultimately develop into mature fruit.

As the fruit develops, its size, texture, aroma, and color change. Depending on the variety and growing conditions, the outside of a ripe mango may be green, yellow, orange, red, or a mixture of several colors.

Once harvested, mangoes can continue to ripen. This makes handling, storage, and transportation important parts of commercial mango production.

A CLOSER LOOK

Why are mangoes so interesting?

Mangoes are interesting partly because the word "mango" describes an enormous range of fruit. Consumers may encounter mangoes that look completely different from one another while still belonging to the same species or closely related cultivated groups.

Flavor

Ripe mango flavor is generally associated with sweetness, acidity, and a distinctive tropical aroma. The balance between these characteristics varies substantially between varieties.

Texture

Texture is another major difference. Some mangoes have very smooth flesh with relatively little fiber, while others contain noticeable strands throughout the fruit. Ripeness also has a major effect on firmness.

Color

Mango skin color is not always a reliable indicator of ripeness. Some varieties remain mostly green even when fully ripe, while others develop yellow, orange, or reddish tones.

Agriculture

Growing mangoes commercially requires attention to climate, soil, irrigation, flowering, pests, diseases, harvesting, and post-harvest handling. Because mango trees are tropical and subtropical plants, temperature is an especially important factor.

Culture

Mangoes have become part of cuisines and cultural traditions across many countries. They can be eaten fresh or incorporated into drinks, desserts, sauces, chutneys, salads, and numerous other dishes.

Global importance

The modern mango industry connects farmers, agricultural researchers, transportation networks, markets, retailers, and consumers. What looks like a simple piece of fruit can therefore represent a surprisingly large agricultural system.

The mango tree

Mango trees are evergreen and can live for many years. Mature trees can become substantial in size, although commercial orchards often manage trees through pruning and other agricultural practices to make harvesting easier.

Ripening

Ripening involves a series of biological changes. The fruit generally becomes softer and develops changes in aroma, flavor, and internal chemistry. These changes are part of what makes a ripe mango different from an immature one.

Mangoes around the world

Today, mangoes are enjoyed in many regions far from their historical origins. International trade has allowed consumers to encounter varieties that may have originated thousands of miles away.

From a botanical perspective, an agricultural perspective, and a cultural perspective, the mango is much more than simply a sweet fruit. It represents thousands of years of cultivation, adaptation, selection, and human interaction with plants.
